## Moral status, responsibility, and rights

Moral status concerns whether an entity matters for its own sake. Moral patienthood concerns being a subject of duties or care; moral agency concerns responsibility. Legal personhood and particular legal rights also require institutional arguments.

For example, a case for assessing possible welfare can justify research while leaving continued deployment to a separate decision. Rejecting legal personhood likewise leaves ethical questions about treatment to be considered.

## Arguments to compare

| Author | Position | Scope |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Long and colleagues | Possible consciousness or robust agency warrants assessment and preparation. | Future systems that might be moral patients. |
| Birhane and van Dijk | Robot-rights discourse can divert attention from human harms and institutional accountability. | The human consequences of rights claims. |
| McClelland | The evidence supports agnosticism about advanced challenger-AI. | Hypothetical advanced systems. |
| Xu Xiangdong | If phenomenal consciousness is the minimum condition, current and near-term agents are unlikely to have moral status. | The article’s abstract. |

## Objections and practical consequences

The debate includes the costs of mistaken attribution, the distribution of moral attention, and responsibility for harms to humans. Bengio and Elmoznino focus on risks of believing AI is conscious. Birhane and van Dijk ask how rights language affects accountability.

Arguments about current systems and arguments about all possible artificial systems have different scopes. The [structured records](../data/evidence.json) retain the premises and objections for each position; [policies and practices](../PRACTICES.md) show how these questions enter decisions about treatment.
