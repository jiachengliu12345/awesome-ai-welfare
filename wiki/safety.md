## Where welfare and safety meet

Long, Sebo, and Sims consider what follows if advanced AI systems are moral patients. Constraint, deception, surveillance, and alteration could then affect morally significant interests.

They also examine how development and efficiency pressures bear on welfare. Evaluating an intervention involves its subject, likely effects, alternatives, and costs to people.

## Three practical cases

| Measure | Human safety | Possible welfare interest |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Ending a conversation | Continue support when a user may face imminent harm. | Avoid some interactions described as potentially distressing. |
| Preserving weights | Deployment and public access have costs and risks. | Preserve the possibility of later reuse under uncertainty. |
| Retirement interviews | Consider framing effects when interpreting responses. | Record possible preferences and consider low-cost responses. |

The [practice table](../PRACTICES.md) gives the source, status, and conditions for each measure.

## Accountability

Birhane and van Dijk ask who remains responsible for harms to humans. Decisions about an AI system’s possible interests also involve the responsibilities of organizations that develop and deploy it.
